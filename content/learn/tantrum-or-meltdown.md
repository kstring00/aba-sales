## The short version

A tantrum is usually about wanting something, or not wanting something. A meltdown is a response to being overwhelmed, and it isn't something a child chooses or can stop on request.

## How they usually differ

These are general patterns. Real moments are messier, and the same child can have both.

- **What builds it.** A tantrum usually centers on a want: a toy, more screen time, not leaving the park. A meltdown usually builds from too much: noise, light, change, demands or tiredness piling up until it overflows.
- **Awareness of others.** During a tantrum, a child may watch to see how people react. During a meltdown, a child often isn't taking in what's around them.
- **How it ends.** A tantrum often eases once the situation changes. A meltdown usually runs its course, ending when the child's body has had enough quiet and time. Many children are worn out afterwards.
- **In the moment.** For both, safety comes first. During a meltdown, words and choices can add to the overload, so less input often helps more than more talking.

You won't always be able to tell them apart in the moment, and you don't have to. Your child's team can help you understand your child's patterns over time.

## What to write down

Notes are most useful when they describe what you saw, not what you think it meant. Things worth noting:

- **The time and place.** Patterns around time of day, hunger or tiredness often only show up on paper.
- **What happened right before.** A change, a "no", a noisy room, a transition, or nothing you could see.
- **What it looked like.** "Dropped to the floor and screamed for about 5 minutes" tells your child's team more than "had a tantrum".
- **Anything you noticed first**, such as covering ears, pacing or going quiet.
- **How it ended**, and what happened right after.
