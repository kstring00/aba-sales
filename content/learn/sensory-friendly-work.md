## What "sensory-friendly" means at work

A sensory-friendly job is one where the sounds, lights, smells, crowds and pace don't wear you out before the work does. It isn't a type of job. Two jobs with the same title can feel completely different depending on the building, the shift and the people. That's why it helps to look at the details of a specific job, not just the job title.

This page is written for young adults looking for work. If you're a parent, it's a good one to read together.

## Things to look at in any job

- **Sound:** Is it loud all day, loud sometimes, or mostly quiet? Is there music, machinery, or a lot of talking?
- **Light:** Bright overhead lights, flickering lights, screens all day, or daylight?
- **Smell:** Food, cleaning products, chemicals, perfume?
- **People:** How many customers or coworkers will you deal with at once? Phone calls or face to face?
- **Pace and predictability:** Same tasks each shift, or something new every hour? Same schedule each week?
- **Instructions:** Written down, or told to you once?
- **Breaks:** Can you step away somewhere quiet?
- **Getting there:** How long is the trip, and is it crowded?

## Questions to ask before you say yes

You can ask these in an interview or after an offer. Asking good questions is normal and shows you're serious.

- "What does a typical shift look like, start to finish?"
- "How loud is the workspace, and is there a quieter place to take breaks?"
- "Are instructions and tasks written down anywhere?"
- "How far ahead is the schedule set, and how often does it change?"
- "Could I see where I'd be working before I start?"

## Changes you can ask for

Many workplaces can make small changes that make a big difference. The Job Accommodation Network, a free national service, lists examples like these:

- **Noise:** noise-canceling headphones, a white noise machine, or a desk away from busy areas.
- **Light:** different lighting, lamps instead of overhead lights, or filters on fluorescent lights.
- **Routine:** written instructions, checklists, a set schedule, or planned breaks.
- **Communication:** instructions by text or email, or one-on-one check-ins instead of group meetings.
- **Time:** timers, reminders, calendars, or extra time while you learn a task.

## Telling an employer

Whether to tell an employer you're autistic or neurodivergent is your choice. You can share a lot, a little, or nothing, and you can decide later. Some people share only what they need: "I work best with written instructions" or "I focus better with headphones." If you'd like help thinking it through, a job coach or a Texas Workforce Vocational Rehabilitation counselor can talk it over with you for free.

## Where to start looking

Some kinds of work often come with quieter or more predictable setups, such as early-morning stocking, inventory, data entry, records and library work, lab and quality-check work, landscaping, animal care, and some remote jobs. It always depends on the specific workplace, so use the questions above. The Jobs page lists hand-picked jobs and hiring programs, each with a plain note on why it's there.
